Caloric intake steering in children

A secondary meta-analysis of how much of an intended energy increase survives in measured intake — the “steering yield”.

Version 1.0 · 22 November 2026 · derived from the frozen feeding-disorders evidence base (v1.1.0) · not a substitute for the main report.

What this adds. The main report asks how much children compensate. This analysis turns the same experiments around: for every 100 kcal a clinician, dietitian or food environment adds, how many are still present in measured intake? The answer is a distribution, not a constant.

1. Definition

Steering yield (SY) is the change in measured energy intake divided by the change in energy made available, expressed as a percentage. It is the complement of compensation: SY = 100 − COMPX. A yield of 100% means every added calorie is eaten; 0% means full compensation; a negative value means the child ate less than at baseline.

The analysis uses three families of controlled experiments already extracted for the main report:

  • Fixed-volume density (k=14): a preload of fixed volume and different energy density is consumed, and intake at the next meal is measured. This is the COMPX pool, one effect per study.
  • Free-volume density (k=7): energy density is changed and the child eats ad libitum over a meal or day. This is the energy-density pool converted to retention per 100 kcal added.
  • Portion size (k=1): the five-day trial raised portion size by 50% and intake by 18%, giving 36% retention (SE 4; 95% CI 28.2–43.8). One trial is shown for completeness, not as a pooled estimate.

All 22 rows are unique by PMID; no study contributes to more than one family. The families are not interchangeable: they differ in horizon, control of volume and degree of experimental control. The overall estimate is therefore a descriptive summary of the same construct measured in different designs, not a single causal effect.

2. Result

Across 22 experiments, the pooled steering yield was 51.4% (95% CI 41.1–61.6), with high heterogeneity (I² 84.6%, τ² 292.4). In plain terms, about half of an intended energy increase was present in measured intake; the interval is compatible with roughly two-fifths to three-fifths.

The two density families are close (51.3% vs 54.1%), and their intervals overlap almost completely. The single portion trial (36.0%, 95% CI 28.2–43.8; k=1) sits inside the multi-day density range (27.5–51.9%) and close to the multi-day pooled yield (36.2%), so there is no evidence that portion size differs from density at the same horizon. The horizon split is clearer: next-meal experiments give 57.0% (95% CI 44.0–70.0; k=17), and multi-day experiments give 36.2% (95% CI 26.1–46.4; k=5). That contrast is confounded with design: all fixed-volume studies are next-meal, so the difference cannot be attributed to horizon alone. The free-volume family contains 3 next-meal and 4 multi-day studies. Practical conclusion: measure total daily intake, not only the next meal.

DL, REML and Paule–Mandel give the same pooled estimate to one decimal place. Leave-one-out estimates ranged from 48.2% to 53.0%, so no single study drives the result. Egger’s test at k=22 gave an intercept of 0.67 (SE 0.99), p=0.49; this does not suggest small-study bias, but the test is low-powered and cannot exclude it.

The 95% prediction interval for a new setting is 14.4% to 88.3%. This is the planning range: a new setting could retain anywhere from near zero to most of the added energy.

4. What the project learned

  1. Plan on a yield, not on the added calories. Adding 100 kcal does not add 100 kcal. The pooled result is about half, but the 14–88% prediction interval is the planning range; measure the child rather than treating the average as a promise.
  2. Free and fixed density are not meaningfully different. The two families agree within a few points. The practical difference is control: a prescribed, measured volume makes the intended addition observable; free access lets the child reduce volume.
  3. Portion size is not demonstrably different from density at the same horizon. The one five-day trial retained about a third of the added energy (36.0%), inside the multi-day density range (27.5–51.9%) and close to the multi-day pooled yield (36.2%); the evidence is thin (k=1).
  4. Horizon and design are confounded. Next-meal experiments give a higher yield than multi-day experiments, but all fixed-volume studies are next-meal; this is not a clean horizon effect. A two-week trial should measure total daily intake, not just the next meal.
  5. Heterogeneity is the message. I² above 80% means the average is a planning prior, not a prediction for an individual child. Measure the child.

5. Limitations

  • The analysis is secondary and uses aggregate study-level effects, not individual participant data.
  • COMPX and energy-density outcomes are not the same measurement; converting both to retention makes them comparable in units but not in causal interpretation.
  • The ED conversion assumes the reported per-10% slope is approximately linear over the density contrasts studied; the main report tests this and finds it adequate but not exact.
  • Portion size is k=1; no pooled portion estimate is claimed.
  • Age meta-regression was not fitted because the ED extraction does not record a common mean age; the COMPX age model is in the main report.
  • The evidence is mostly short-term and in healthy children; clinical populations, including WHS, are extrapolations.
