The central finding is quantitative and consistent across two different paradigms: children do not eat “one calorie for one calorie” after a denser feed. In acute preload experiments about 49% of the extra energy is offset by lower later intake, so roughly 51% is retained. In controlled energy-density studies, a 10% rise in dietary energy density raises measured intake by about 5.4% — the same order of compensation, derived without a preload paradigm. This is the number to plan against, not the assumption that a denser feed is fully compensated.

The clinical corollary matters more than the average. Compensation is incomplete but substantial, and it is larger when the added energy arrives as a large volume, as carbohydrate, or as a meal that closely precedes the next meal. It is smaller when energy arrives as fat in a small volume, as a liquid between meals, or through a tube, where delivered energy is guaranteed but oral intake may fall. That is the basis of the ranked strategy table in §4.

1. Caloric compensation: what it is and why it is the target

Caloric compensation is the reduction in later intake that follows energy eaten earlier. It is measured with a preload paradigm: the child receives a low-energy and a high-energy version of the same food or drink, and intake at a later test meal is weighed. The standard summary is the compensation index:

COMPX (%) = [(meal after low-energy preload − meal after high-energy preload) ÷ (high-energy preload − low-energy preload)] × 100

COMPX of 100% means the child eats exactly less at the next meal to offset the preload; 0% means no adjustment at all; negative values mean the child eats more after the denser preload. The index is acute and single-meal by construction; it is not a 24-hour intake measure.

Three mechanisms explain why the same number of calories can produce different compensation:

  • Volume and gastric distension. A large-volume feed distends the stomach and triggers satiation more than a small, dense feed. This is why concentrating a formula can leave total energy unchanged when the child drinks to a volume target — the pattern seen in the index case.
  • Macronutrient. In controlled preload studies, fat suppresses later intake less than carbohydrate joule-for-joule; the high-fat preload produced weaker compensation in restrained and unrestrained adults [3]. Fat also has a lower thermic effect and a weaker effect on some satiety signals.
  • Food form and sensory-specific satiety. Liquids are less satiating than solids at matched energy [4]. Within a meal, liking for the eaten food falls relative to other foods — sensory-specific satiety [5]. Offering a variety of foods can therefore increase total intake without a change in energy density [6].

The size of the compensation effect is not fixed. It varies with age, weight status, the interval between preload and meal, the preload’s physical form, and the palatability and variety of the test meal. That heterogeneity is large (I² 72.4% in the pooled COMPX model), and it is the reason the map in §3 does not use one number for every disorder. The practical question is not “does this child compensate?” but “how much of the added energy survives?” — and the answer is condition- and route-specific.

Reading the numbers. A pooled COMPX of 47% means that for every 100 kcal added to a feed, about 53 kcal remain in daily intake if the acute relationship holds over the day. That last clause is an extrapolation. The direct daily-intake trials in §2.4 give a similar answer, which is reassuring but not identical: they measure a different question (a change in the diet over one meal or five days) in a different population (mostly preschool children).

In plain words. After a rich drink, a child eats less at the next meal — but not enough to cancel it. About half to two-thirds of the extra calories stay. A small, fatty feed is harder to compensate than a large, sugary one.

2. Meta-analysis: how much energy survives?

2.1 Question and eligibility

We asked two questions. Primary: in children, what proportion of a preload’s energy is compensated at the next meal (COMPX)? Secondary: when the energy density of the diet is changed experimentally, what percentage change in energy intake follows per 10% change in energy density? The second question does not use a preload and therefore provides a check with a different experimental paradigm, not an independent one.

Eligible studies were experimental or controlled crossover studies in humans that (a) manipulated the energy content or energy density of a preload, meal or daily diet, (b) measured subsequent or total energy intake objectively, and (c) reported a mean and a measure of variance, or enough data to derive them. Animal studies, mechanistic studies without intake outcomes, and studies in which intake was not measured were excluded. 2.2 Primary result: COMPX in children

Nine studies contributed one effect each (14 rows before collapsing dependent observations). The pooled compensation index was 48.7% (DerSimonian–Laird 95% CI 32.9–60.4%; Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman 95% CI 35.2–62.3%). Heterogeneity was high (I² 72.4%, τ² 184.8), and the 95% prediction interval was wide (6.0 to 87.3), meaning that a new study in a new population could plausibly find anything from no compensation to near-complete compensation. The implied retention is 51.3 kcal per 100 kcal added (HKSJ 37.7–64.8), an extrapolation from the acute index.

The failure-to-thrive study is the one clinically distinct cohort: 1-year-old children with weight gain in the lowest 5% showed a mean COMPX of −19.5%, i.e. they ate slightly more after the high-energy drink, while matched controls compensated 64.2% [8]. With only 27 cases this estimate is imprecise, but its direction is important: undernourished children are not necessarily better compensators, and a dense feed may add energy rather than displace it. The sensitivity analysis excluding this cohort raises the pooled estimate to 50.4%, so the main conclusion does not depend on it.

Age subgroup estimates were unstable: preschool 64.9% (k=4), school-age 39.9% (k=3), infancy 11.3% (k=2); the between-group test was nominally significant (p=0.014) but the subgroups contain two to four studies each, and a continuous meta-regression of COMPX on mean age found no slope (1.8 percentage points per year, 95% CI −4.8 to 8.5; p=0.53). We do not interpret the age subgroups as a developmental law. They are consistent with the infant study, in which compensation fell between 11 and 15 months [11]. They are also consistent with the variability across ages reported in the meta-analysis [19].

2.3 Secondary result: energy density and measured intake

Seven studies changed the energy density of foods or meals and measured intake. The pooled ratio (summary slope) was +5.41% energy intake per +10% energy density (HKSJ 95% CI 3.27–7.56%; I² 92.2%; 95% prediction interval −1.6 to 12.4). The prediction interval crosses zero: a new study could plausibly find no relationship at all. Three of the seven studies reduced density and four increased it, so the slope is applied symmetrically as an assumption, not a measured symmetry. If a 10% density increase were fully expressed as 10% more energy at constant weight, compensation would be 0%; the measured 5.4% implies about 46% compensation (HKSJ 24–67%), closely overlapping the preload estimate.

The five-day trial is the most clinically relevant design because it tests whether compensation appears over days. It did not: the higher-density condition raised intake by 84 kcal/day and the lower-density condition lowered it by 72 kcal/day, with no convergence over five days [24]. A separate five-day trial found that a 50% portion increase raised daily intake by 167 kcal (18%) without adjustment [27]. Two other designs point the same way: a 2 × 2 portion-size × energy-density study found independent, additive effects on meal intake [28], and a secondary analysis of 6,355 meals in 94 preschool children found intake rising by about 113 kcal per unit of consumed energy density before decelerating at very high density [29]. In other words, one five-day trial (n=49) found no convergence over five days, and the same pattern is visible in real menus; a single trial is not proof of robustness.

Scale-free sensitivity. Because the ED contrasts ranged from 20% to 275% and I² was 92.2%, the linear “% per +10% ED” slope is an approximation. A scale-free elasticity model, ln(EI ratio)/ln(ED ratio), gives a pooled β of 0.57 (HKSJ 0.39–0.75); at +10% ED this implies 5.6% higher intake and 55.8% retention (37.9–74.1), close to the linear 54.1%. The clinical/LMIC subgroup (Sanchez-Griñan, Islam) has β 0.43, implying 41.5% retention; the healthy-preschool subgroup has β 0.62, implying 60.7%. For the index child, the free-volume estimate could therefore be closer to 42% than 54%; the fixed-volume COMPX estimate is unchanged. The trial-level elasticities are in Table 2i.

2.4 What the meta-analysis does and does not establish

It establishes that compensation is incomplete in controlled settings, that the two designs converge on roughly 35–45% compensation, and that the effect is not explained away by the failure-to-thrive cohort or by the choice of one effect per study.

It does not establish a daily kcal effect in a specific child. COMPX is an acute single-meal preload measure in mostly healthy children; the pooled estimate is not a measured 24-hour effect. The energy-density trials are mostly single meals in preschool children, with one five-day trial. Neither body of evidence includes children with Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome, and only one small cohort is undernourished. The extrapolation to the index case is a hypothesis to test with intake and growth data, not a prescription.

Heterogeneity and bias. I² was 72.4% for COMPX and 92.2% for energy density. Sources include preload form, interval, age, weight status, test-meal palatability and the number of foods manipulated. All included studies used within-subject designs, which controls between-child variability but not order or carry-over effects; most were small (n=31–236). Publication bias could not be assessed reliably with 14 studies; the funnel plot is therefore descriptive. Egger’s test was pre-specified only for k≥10 and was not run.

Preload form. In plain words. Nine small studies point the same way. The average child cancels about 47% of extra calories, but the range is wide. The studies were short and mostly in healthy children, so treat the number as a planning guide, not a promise.

Same-meal milk manipulation. In 125 preschool children, serving higher-energy-density milk at lunch did not change total meal energy overall, but the response differed by sex: boys reduced food intake by 43±8 kcal and held meal energy constant, whereas girls did not reduce food and meal energy rose 24±10 kcal [66]. For the index boy, whose milk is being concentrated, this is a direct reason to measure food displacement at the same meal rather than assume it away; it does not establish a preload COMPX.

What the clinical studies show. The direction is consistent across most populations: when density rises, energy intake or weight gain rises in most studies that measured it. The nulls matter: the preterm and normal-infant studies found similar energy intake when volume rose on the dilute feed, and the isocaloric tube crossover found equal growth with higher oral intake on the dilute feed. Tolerance is the limiting factor: the high-energy CHD trials reported more feeding intolerance (abdominal distension, gastric retention, diarrhoea) and constipation. In faltering growth, a nutrient-dense formula and an energy-supplemented formula of the same density produced the same volume and energy intake, so the benefit of the nutrient-dense feed was protein and micronutrient quality, not extra calories [45]. In congenital heart disease, five RCTs and a 2025 meta-analysis show a weight-gain advantage of about 276 g over standard formula [57], and the individual trials show less weight loss or better weight z-scores [47][48][49][50]. When volume was fixed — glucose polymer added to the same feed, or equal volumes of 1.0 and 1.5 kcal/ml by tube — energy intake rose in proportion to density [51][52][53]. The volume-intolerance study is an uncontrolled before-after series of 10 children, not a randomised trial; it is included for the direction and the tolerance signal, not for effect size.

The closest analogues to the index child. Two of the seven energy-density trials are closer to this child than the healthy-preschool set. In Sanchez-Griñan 1992, hospitalised young children recovering from malnutrition received 0.5 vs 1.0 kcal/g diets; the dense diet raised energy intake to 148 vs 110 kcal/kg/day despite a smaller weight of food, and the authors attributed the limit on the dilute diet to gastric capacity [25]. In Islam 2006, breastfed 9–18-month-olds received complementary food at 0.4 vs 1.5 kcal/g; the dense food reduced breast-milk intake from 234 to 192 g/day, but total energy still rose from 441 to 774 kcal/day (+75%) [26]. That is partial milk displacement — the index pattern in a free-volume trial — and it is a reason to measure oral displacement rather than assume it away.

What the clinical studies do not show. A formal pooled ratio of energy intake or volume intake is not estimable from this literature. Only one study reports both energy and fluid means with a variance [44]; a second reports the direction of volume change without numeric means [55]; the rest report growth, percent of requirement, or a design-defined contrast. Pooling those ratios would require imputing variances, which would manufacture precision the literature does not have. We therefore report the ratios descriptively. Where volume was fixed, the energy ratio was 1.32–1.50 with a volume ratio of 1.00 [51][52][53]. Where volume was free, the energy ratio fell toward 1.00 as volume rose on the dilute feed [55][56], and 27% of infants in the failure-to-thrive study held energy constant by reducing volume [43]. The oral-supplement trial is intermediate: both formats supplied 300 kcal per serving, and the between-group difference was 531 kcal/day at day 28 [44]. Both arms received 300 kcal per serving (2.4 kcal/ml, 125 ml vs 1.5 kcal/ml, 200 ml); the +531 kcal/day difference is between a dense low-volume and a standard ONS, not versus no supplement, so it does not measure retention. It is an industry-funded pilot in 5.8-year-olds, not a dose for this child.

Clinical bottom line for a child who defends energy and drops volume. Density alone is not a reliable way to add calories when the child is free to reduce volume: the compensation literature and the faltering-growth switch study both show that some children will defend energy and let volume fall. A small unblinded crossover in tube-fed children found that oral intake was 20–30% higher on the lower-density overnight feed mainly in boys, with no effect in girls [54]; for this boy, that is a concrete reason to expect some oral displacement when the overnight feed is concentrated, and to measure it rather than assume it away. The reliable route is to fix the volume and raise density — a prescribed tube feed, or a measured oral volume with a denser formula — and then protect free water. This is the evidence base for the plan in Section 7: set a volume target, choose the density that meets the energy target at that volume, and measure both energy intake and hydration rather than assuming that a denser feed means more calories.

2.11 What happens after the next meal: whole-day retention

COMPX stops at the next meal. Pooling the three paediatric trials that measured whole-day intake over 5–8 days gives a retention of 33% (95% CI 14–52, I² 20%), against 51% for the next-meal pool [67][68][69]. The gap is measurement scope, not elapsed time: the constant-elasticity estimate is about 0.43 for whole-day studies and 0.72 for single-meal studies. For the index child, whole-day retention is the central planning figure; the next-meal +128 kcal/day at 1.22 kcal/ml is the optimistic case, and the whole-day range is about +82 to +105 kcal/day. A 24-hour retention estimate is not available: the within-child data show no second wave of adjustment, so the lower whole-day figure should not be read as compensation accumulating over days [70]. A 7-day baseline versus a 14-day trial detects about 145 kcal/day at a within-child CV of 19%; a 3-day baseline cannot.

3. The disorder map

“Feeding disorder” is an umbrella, not a diagnosis. The map below separates the conditions that most often present with low oral intake and links each to the mechanism that limits intake, the first caloric route that is likely to work, and the point at which oral strategies should give way to tube feeding. It is deliberately mechanism-first: two children with different diagnoses but the same intake limiter often need the same strategy, and two children with the same diagnosis but different limiters do not.

How to read the map. Start with the column “what limits intake”, not the diagnosis. If the limit is safety (aspiration), texture and route come first. If it is motility (gastroparesis, severe reflux), small volume and sometimes lower fat come first. If it is sensory (ARFID, autism), predictable preferred foods and behavioural work come first. If it is feeding skill (oral motor weakness), texture modification and oral motor therapy come first, with a tube when fatigue or aspiration persists. If it is energy need (CHD, CF, SBS), treat the cause first — cardiac feeding support, enzymes in CF, modular or continuous feeding in SBS — then density and tube supplementation. If it is inflammation or obstruction (EoE, stricture), treat the lesion before escalating calories.

3.1 The three axes that decide the route

Safety first. If there is coughing, choking, wet voice, recurrent pneumonia or documented aspiration, the first intervention is texture and positioning, not calories. A denser feed is useless if it is aspirated. The nutritional study of children with dysphagia found that 39.8% did not meet energy expenditure and that feeding devices were associated with lower intake, not higher — the route had been chosen for safety, not optimised for energy [103].

Motility second. Gastroparesis and severe reflux limit volume per feed. In these children the density strategy must be delivered in small volumes, and fat should be increased cautiously because fat slows gastric emptying. A liquid energy supplement between meals may be better tolerated than a larger meal. The high ARFID prevalence in gastroparesis and functional dyspepsia (48.5–66.7%) is a reminder that a behavioural label can sit on top of a motility problem [82].

Growth risk third. The lower the weight-for-age and the longer the inadequate intake, the earlier tube feeding should be discussed. A trial of oral density is reasonable for days to weeks, not months, when weight is falling away from the curve. The multidisciplinary programmes that combine behavioural work with medical and dietetic care report 71% tube weaning in complex cohorts, and a separate meta-analysis reports 67–69% full weaning — evidence that tube dependence is treatable, not a life sentence [102][101].

4.1 Why fat is the preferred way to raise density, and why “just add oil” is not the whole answer

Fat is the most energy-dense macronutrient and, in controlled comparisons, suppresses later intake less than carbohydrate joule-for-joule [3]. A preload study that varied physical state and fat content found that solid preloads were more satiating than liquid preloads, and that the fat content did not produce a compensatory reduction over the rest of the day [113]. This is the physiological basis for preferring a small volume of fat-rich feed over a larger volume of carbohydrate-rich feed. Energy-density trials in children point the same way, although trials that isolate fat are few [114][115][24].

But fat is not free of effects. It slows gastric emptying, can worsen reflux and steatorrhoea, and adds cost and palatability problems. In gastroparesis and severe reflux the order reverses: small volume first, fat second. In short bowel and cholestatic liver disease, MCT may be better absorbed than long-chain fat. The strategy table is therefore a default, not a protocol.

4.2 The between-meal principle

The strongest clinical trial evidence for weight gain with oral nutritional supplements is not the same as evidence for retention: the trials below measure growth over months, not 24-hour displacement. A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs in undernourished children found +0.423 kg weight and +0.417 cm height versus control at up to six months [106]. A second meta-analysis of 10 RCTs in faltering growth found +0.4 kg and +0.3 cm, with a weighted supplement contribution of 412 kcal, 16.3 g protein and 395 ml per day [107]. A trial in picky-eating children at nutritional risk found improved growth with ONS plus dietary counselling [59]. A 2025 meta-analysis of five RCTs in 874 picky-eating or underweight children found moderate evidence for ONS plus dietary counselling on weight, with no excess adverse events; it still does not report retention [116]. A separate 28-day RCT compared two ONS formats with equal 300 kcal per serving (2.4 kcal/ml, 125 ml vs 1.5 kcal/ml, 200 ml) and found +531 kcal/day total intake in the dense low-volume arm [44]. That supports denser, smaller servings being consumed in greater total energy; it is not retention versus no supplement. The effects are modest but consistent. They do not establish that the supplement is retained; that is why ONS is not ranked on the retention scale and sits with the not-estimable rows.

The counter-example is instructive. A tertiary feeding clinic withdrew high-energy oral supplements in 38 children who were taking about two-thirds of their daily energy requirements as supplements. By follow-up, 79% had stopped all supplements, mean weight SDS was stable (0.08, SD 0.6), and 17% showed catch-up weight gain — suggesting that in some children the supplement was displacing food rather than adding to it [108]. The lesson is not that ONS do not work; it is that timing and volume decide whether they add or substitute. Give them between meals, in small volumes, and track total intake rather than supplement volume.

4.3 Appetite stimulants: where they fit

Cyproheptadine is the most studied agent. A systematic review of 46 articles across 21 populations found significant weight gain in 39, with transient sedation the commonest adverse effect; benefit was minimal in malignant disease and HIV [109]. In cystic fibrosis, a retrospective cohort of 45 children found improvements in weight and BMI z-scores after at least three months [110]. The Cochrane review found only low-certainty evidence, so firm conclusions about routine use cannot be drawn [89]. In ARFID, mirtazapine has retrospective support for shorter hospital stay and weight gain, but no randomised trial [77][76]. A 2026 RCT in 2–10-year-olds found low-dose cyproheptadine gave BMI gain comparable to the standard dose with less drowsiness; it did not include children under 2 and does not change the label barrier [117]. None is labelled at 18 months. The Peritol PL SmPC says cyproheptadine should not be used under 2 years (§4.2), and separately lists cachectic or wasted patients as a §4.3 contraindication; a child with faltering growth who meets SAM criteria may fall under that contraindication regardless of age. The mirtazapine PL SmPC says it should not be used under 18 years (§4.2), with a §4.4 suicidality warning; this is not a §4.3 contraindication. Megestrol has no indication at 18 months; the PL label starts at 12 years for cancer/AIDS anorexia (Table 4b). These drugs are adjuncts when oral and behavioural strategies have plateaued; they do not replace the need to treat the underlying feeding problem.

4.4 Tube feeding is a caloric strategy, not a failure

A feeding tube guarantees delivered energy, not total energy: oral intake may fall when the tube starts or increases, so net total intake must be measured. That is its advantage and its risk: it can deliver the prescribed energy reliably, but it can also entrench tube dependence. ESPGHAN recommends enteral nutrition when oral intake is inadequate and the gut works, with intragastric and intermittent feeding preferred when possible [104]. Its 2021 PEG update addresses access technique and complications, including a removal criterion after 8–12 weeks of oral intake, but does not set a weaning protocol [118]; no ESPGHAN position paper specifically on PFD or tube weaning was found in the 2020–2026 search. In a cohort of 76 tube-dependent children with ARFID, tube-feeding schedule was associated with the probability of any oral intake — schedule is a modifiable part of the treatment, not an administrative detail [105]. Population-based data on gastrostomy in congenital anomalies show that tube use varies widely by condition and country, a reminder that local practice, not only diagnosis, shapes the decision [119]. The paediatric tube-weaning meta-analysis reports 67–69% full weaning after treatment, with multidisciplinary and behavioural components associated with success [101]. A 2025 scoping review of 45 studies found varied, mostly interdisciplinary weaning strategies and no single best protocol [120].

What not to do. Do not respond to poor weight gain by progressively diluting feeds to increase volume, and do not assume that a higher-calorie formula will be fully compensated. Both mistakes are common. The first floods the child with water and displaces energy; the second assumes the opposite of what the data show. Measure intake, weight and hydration together.

In plain words. Add fat to small amounts, add calories between meals, keep portions and variety up, treat the cause, and use a tube when oral intake cannot keep up.

5. Water: the constraint that concentrated feeds create

Every increase in energy density is also a decrease in the water-to-energy ratio of the feed. In a child who is already small and has limited reserve, that trade can produce hypernatraemia, constipation, poor perfusion or uraemia before it produces weight gain. Water is therefore not an afterthought to the caloric plan; it is a co-primary target.

5.1 Maintenance water

The Holliday–Segar method remains the standard bedside estimate for maintenance water in children: 100 ml/kg/day for the first 10 kg, 50 ml/kg/day for the next 10 kg, and 20 ml/kg/day above 20 kg. It estimates water need, not free-water deficit; in illness, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, high-output stools or diuresis, losses must be added. For the index case, 6 kg × 100 ml/kg/day = 600 ml/day of total water from all sources, not 600 ml of free water. The estimate is a starting point, not a universal requirement: reviews of water requirements emphasise that needs vary with climate, activity, diet and illness, and that there is no single intake that fits every child [111][112][121]. Reviews of water intake make the same point: needs vary, and a single universal intake is not supported [122][111].

5.3 Renal solute load

The potential renal solute load (PRSL) of a feed is the sum of nitrogen (as urea), sodium, potassium, chloride and phosphorus. It determines the osmotic load the kidney must excrete and therefore the urine volume needed to stay in balance. Standard infant formulas have a PRSL of about 135–177 mOsm/L (20–26 mOsm/100 kcal), a margin that is adequate at normal dilution but narrows when the feed is concentrated or water intake falls [124][125][126]. Concentrating a 0.67 kcal/ml formula to 1.0 kcal/ml raises the solute load per millilitre by about 50%, but the solute load per 100 kcal is unchanged: the same protein and electrolytes travel with the same calories. Density changes the water that accompanies each kcal, not the daily osmotic load. At the measured 590 kcal/day the load is about 118–153 mOsm/day; at 840 kcal/day it is 168–218 mOsm/day, which at a urine osmolality of 600–700 mOsm/kg needs roughly 240–360 ml/day of urine (1.7–2.5 ml/kg/h) at the top of the energy target, and about 170–255 ml/day at 590 kcal/day. In a child with fever, diarrhoea or poor intake, that margin can disappear. Preterm infants have a different renal solute load from term infants [127]. Separately, a high urea load can drive osmotic diuresis and hypernatraemia independently of sodium intake [128].

PRSL is calculated as: nitrogen (mg/L) ÷ 28 + Na (mmol/L) + K (mmol/L) + Cl (mmol/L) + P (mmol/L). It is a property of the feed, not a measurement in the child; the child’s urine osmolality and serum sodium are the check [125][124]. The clinical review of renal solute load in infancy reaches the same conclusion: conventional formulas are safe at standard dilution, but feedings at the upper limits of protein and electrolyte concentration do not have a satisfactory margin [126].

The 1971, 1987 and 1999 water and renal-solute sources are cited from their published full text; PubMed holds no abstract for them, and their bibliographic records were verified by esummary (title, journal, year).

5.4 Monitoring

  • Urine output 1–2 ml/kg/hour is a practical target at lower energy; when the catch-up goal is above about 110 kcal/kg, the higher solute load needs about 1.5–2.5 ml/kg/hour (9–15 ml/hour at 6 kg).
  • Urine specific gravity roughly 1.005–1.015; values up to about 1.020 may be normal when solute load is high, so interpret with urine output and weight.
  • Serum sodium at baseline and after any density change; a rising sodium is a confirmatory, late signal — weight loss, oliguria and high specific gravity usually precede it. Watch for dilutional hyponatraemia (sodium <135 mmol/L), especially with antiseizure medication; check sodium at baseline, 24–72 h after a density change, then weekly.
  • Weight, stool and feeding tolerance daily during a density change; constipation and hard stools are common when water is inadequate. In gastrostomy-fed children, dehydration and low free-water intake are part of the risk profile for urolithiasis [129].
  • Aspiration risk. In children with dysphagia, more thin liquid can increase reflux and chest X-rays; a retrospective study of blenderized tube feeds found that patients receiving more thin liquid had more chest X-rays [130]. Free water still matters, but the route and consistency must respect the swallow.

The silent trade. A feed can be “more calorific” and still deliver fewer net calories if the child drinks less, vomits more, or becomes dehydrated and anorexic. The index case’s pattern — energy held constant while volume fell on a denser formula — is exactly this trade. The fix is not another density increase in isolation; it is density plus a fixed volume target, free water, and a route that does not depend on the child’s volume regulation.

In plain words. A denser feed contains less water. A 6 kg child still needs about 600 ml a day. If the feed is concentrated and the child drinks less, give more free water between feeds and watch urine and sodium.

6. Refeeding risk when intake rises

Refeeding syndrome is a shift of phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells after a period of reduced intake, driven by insulin release and anabolic recovery. It can cause weakness, rhabdomyolysis, arrhythmia, seizures, heart failure and death. The risk is concentrated in the most severely malnourished child and in the first days of repletion [131][132].

In paediatric intensive care, an incidence of up to 7.4% has been reported in children receiving nutritional support [132]. The ASPEN consensus recommends risk stratification before feeding, correction of electrolytes before advancement, and a deliberate ramp rather than a rapid increase [131]. In anorexia nervosa, the older “start low and go slow” doctrine is being challenged by higher-calorie protocols, but the monitoring principle is unchanged: the safe speed depends on the child’s risk, not on a universal number [91][133].

7. Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome and the index case

7.1 What the syndrome adds

Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome (WHS) is a contiguous-gene deletion syndrome of 4p16.3, with an estimated frequency of 1 in 20,000–50,000 births. The core phenotype includes prenatal-onset growth deficiency, hypotonia, developmental delay, seizures and characteristic facial features; 80% of a 87-patient series had prenatal growth deficiency followed by short stature and slow weight gain [93]. Growth should be plotted on WHS-specific charts, not WHO or local charts; charts exist for 0–4 years and now to 18 years [95][96]. Growth hormone has been used in selected children with severe short stature, but it is not routine and the evidence is case-level [98]. Loss-of-function variants in NSD2 (WHSC1), which lies within the WHS critical region, have been associated with failure to thrive and a milder WHS phenotype, which is relevant when counselling about growth [134].

Feeding difficulty is a recognised part of the phenotype. Reviews and case series describe feeding difficulties in WHS [135][136], often attributed to hypotonia [136]. Dysphagia has been documented in a functional series [137]. Gastrostomy has been used in severe cases, including a published anaesthetic case of a 3-month-old undergoing laparoscopic gastrostomy [97]. We did not find a syndrome-specific prevalence estimate for feeding disorder or tube feeding in the indexed literature; the statement that feeding difficulty is common is supported, but a precise percentage is not. That is a gap, not a negative finding.

At 6 kg and 18 months, severe acute malnutrition cannot be excluded until length, weight-for-length and MUAC are measured; if WHO criteria are met, the WHO stabilisation pathway takes precedence over the density trial.

Before changing water or feed density, confirm: renal ultrasound, renal function and urinalysis (urinary tract malformations occur in WHS); electrolytes including sodium and bicarbonate; cardiac status and any fluid restriction (congenital heart lesions are common in WHS); and weight-for-length, MUAC and oedema. In renal or cardiac disease, 600 ml/day is a starting estimate, not a target, and the free-water figure must be recalculated [93].

A qualification that must precede the plan. The family’s WHS briefing concluded that under-eating was not the explanation for this child’s growth pattern, and placed the focus on programmed growth, absorption and growth-hormone testing. Nothing in this analysis overturns that conclusion. In WHS, 80% of a large series had prenatal-onset growth deficiency [93]. The extended growth curves show the same prenatal and early-life pattern [96]. The current intake of 98 kcal/kg/day is already high per kilogram. More intake may not convert into linear growth. The caloric plan below is therefore a test of whether absorbable energy is limiting, not an assumption that it is. If weight does not respond despite documented intake, the next questions are absorption (stool, pancreatic function, celiac serology), the growth-hormone axis, and syndrome-specific growth expectations — not another density increase.

7.2 The compensation pattern in this child

The index case is an 18-month-old boy with WHS, weight about 6 kg, taking about 590 kcal/day. The milk component is 500 ml: 80 ml at 1.0 kcal/ml (80 kcal) plus 420 ml at 0.67 kcal/ml (281 kcal), giving 361 kcal from milk and about 229 kcal from solids. The observed pattern is that when the formula is concentrated, energy intake is held roughly constant while the volume drunk falls. The 361 kcal uses the measured average of 0.722 kcal/ml; at the rounded label value of 0.72 the same 500 ml is 360 kcal, a 1 kcal difference that does not change any bound. In COMPX terms, that is close to full compensation for the formula change — higher than the pooled 48.7% — and it is the reason further concentration alone has not worked.

The energy goal is not a fixed 110–140 kcal/kg/day. The length-based catch-up formula is stated as catch-up kcal/day = DRI for chronological age (kcal/kg/day) × median weight for the child’s length (kg); divide by actual weight for the per-kg form [138]. Homan’s DRI values are an older RDA-based table (108, 98 and 102 kcal/kg/day for 0–6, 6–12 and 1–3 years); WHO/FAO 2004 or IOM energy requirements for 1–2 years are lower, about 82.4 kcal/kg/day for boys and 80.1 for girls aged 1–2 years, and give lower targets. An age-based variant uses ideal weight for age instead of weight for length [139]; at 18 months, 82 kcal/kg × WHO median 10.9 kg / 6 kg ≈ 150 kcal/kg ≈ 890 kcal/day, above the 840 ceiling. The age-based variant therefore overstates the need in a child with programmed short stature. Use weight-for-length, ideally against WHS growth references, and treat 890 kcal/day only as an illustration of the age-based assumption, not a target. The child’s length is required and is not yet recorded here, so the goal cannot be calculated from this report. Consequently the 840 kcal/day ceiling and the 1.22 kcal/ml milk density are conditional on that goal, not independent prescriptions. A 1.22 kcal/ml feed is also not a standard product density: it requires dietitian-led fortification and electrolyte monitoring.

The index-child bound at 1.22 kcal/ml. With the milk volume fixed at 500 ml, the only source of compensation is the solid-food component, about 229 kcal/day. At 1.22 kcal/ml the milk supplies 610 kcal, so the density change adds 249 kcal (610 − 361 = 249). If the child drops all solids, total intake is 610 kcal, a net gain of +20 kcal/day over the current 590; if no solids are dropped, total intake is 839 kcal, a net gain of +249. At the next-meal retention of 51% the expected net gain is +128 kcal/day; at the 33–42% seen over the whole day it is +82 to +105 kcal/day. The whole-day figure is the central planning range; +128 is the optimistic case. If free volume is used instead, the clinical/LMIC elasticity subgroup implies 42% retention, so the same 249 kcal would yield about +105 kcal/day; the fixed-volume calculation is the one that matches the prescribed 500 ml. The 840 kcal/day ceiling is not exceeded even at full solids (839 kcal). This is the range the two-week trial is designed to measure; it is not a promise that the top of the range will be reached.

The arithmetic makes the central point: a density increase is worth about 74 kcal/day at the pooled compensation rate (48.7%) if the child accepts the same volume, and it is worth nothing if the child defends energy intake and drops volume. A between-meal supplement is a second route to roughly the same 74 kcal, but a supplement is itself a liquid preload; this child may compensate for it as readily as for denser formula. The two routes should be treated as alternative hypotheses to test with the same two-week measurement, not as one being superior. The one route that guarantees delivered energy is tube feeding, although oral intake may fall, so net total intake must be measured, and that is why it is the escalation route rather than a parallel lifestyle change. When energy is held, feed volume plus free water is 654 ml/day at the current regimen, 654 ml/day at 1.0 kcal/ml and 660 ml/day at 1.5 kcal/ml: density moves water from the feed into a cup, it does not reduce the child’s total fluid load. At the same volume, total fluid rises (675 ml at 1.0 and 725 ml at 1.5). The benefit is energy per millilitre within a fixed water ceiling, not volume sparing.

7.3 A sequence for the care team

Faltering-growth thresholds and the tube-feeding gate (NICE NG75). NICE NG75 uses thresholds for concern on the UK WHO growth charts, where a centile space is the gap between adjacent centile lines: a fall across 1 or more weight centile spaces if birthweight was below the 9th centile, 2 or more if birthweight was between the 9th and 91st, 3 or more if above the 91st, or a current weight below the 2nd centile for age whatever the birthweight. In WHS, the growth judgement uses the WHS charts and the Step 6 numeric triggers, not standard-chart spaces; the NICE gate is applied as a process requirement (serious concern about poor weight gain, appropriate specialist multidisciplinary assessment for possible causes and contributory factors, other interventions tried without improvement). The child’s birthweight is not stated here, so only the <2nd-centile branch can be applied without it. NICE says to consider enteral tube feeding only after those conditions, with goals and a withdrawal strategy, including progressive reduction with strategies to promote oral intake (NG75 §1.2.1 and §1.2.25–1.2.26, published 27 September 2017, accessed 19 November 2026, nice.org.uk/guidance/ng75). This is consistent with the 4–6 week gastrostomy discussion in Step 6, not a substitute for it.

Two-week oral trial, if the oral route is safe. First measure weight-for-length/BMI, MUAC and oedema and state whether WHO severe acute malnutrition (SAM) criteria are met. If SAM, start the WHO stabilisation pathway (F-75 at about 100 kcal/kg/day, antibiotics, micronutrients) and do not start density escalation until rehabilitation. If SAM is excluded, start at the current tolerated intake (590 kcal/day, 98 kcal/kg), not below it. Treat as high risk if weight-for-length/BMI z is below −3, weight loss exceeds 10%, intake has been negligible for more than 7 days, or baseline phosphate/potassium/magnesium is low; until weight-for-length/BMI and MUAC are measured, manage him as high risk. If low risk, ramp the milk over 3–5 days from 0.72 to 1.0 to 1.2 kcal/ml at a fixed 500 ml (1.22 kcal/ml is the milk ceiling at 500 ml; 840 kcal/day is the full-solids total, not an independent limit); if high risk, advance by 10–20 kcal/kg every 1–2 days with phosphate/K/Mg at baseline, 24 h and 48–72 h. The target is the catch-up goal in §7.2, provisionally bounded at 840 kcal/day; do not exceed 840 kcal/day and do not add ONS during the trial. Keep solids unchanged and start free water at 50–100 ml/day and titrate to urine output, specific gravity and sodium; the maximum at this density is about 200–250 ml/day. Give it in supervised 10–20 ml aliquots and stop for cough, wet voice or fatigue. Do not thicken water as a substitute for free water: thickener adds carbohydrate and solute. If the swallow is unsafe, use tube flushes or a lower feed density instead. Measure weight and urine daily in week 1, and sodium at baseline, 24–72 h and weekly. Stop and move to NG/gastrostomy if weight gain is <5 g/kg/day over 14 days (<30 g/day at 6 kg), weight velocity is <−2 z-score or declines ≥1 z-score on the WHS chart, weight falls by more than 5% in a week, or intolerance develops [1][2]. Under full compensation the expected gain is 0 kcal/day; the trial is a test, not a treatment.

  1. Measure before changing. Three-day weighed intake, weight on the WHS chart, length/head circumference, weight-for-length/BMI, MUAC, oedema, urine output, baseline phosphate/potassium/magnesium/sodium. State whether WHO severe acute malnutrition criteria are met; if they are, use the WHO stabilisation pathway alongside the ASPEN bundle. Confirm what is milk, what is solid, and what is supplement.
  2. Hold the volume, raise the density modestly. If the milk is to be concentrated, do it under dietitian supervision and keep the volume target fixed for two weeks. Add fat/MCT rather than more carbohydrate where tolerated. Do not concentrate and increase volume at the same time; you will not know which failed.
  3. Add free water. At the current pattern, about 154 ml/day is the maximum supplemental water if solids contain none. If the milk is concentrated to 1.0 kcal/ml and energy is held at 361 kcal, milk volume falls to about 361 ml and the maximum free water rises to about 293 ml/day; at 1.5 kcal/ml, milk volume about 241 ml and maximum free water about 419 ml/day. These are upper bounds, not targets: start at 50–100 ml/day and titrate to urine output, specific gravity and sodium, watching for dilutional hyponatraemia. Before any oral water, obtain SLP/OT assessment and a documented consistency plan; give 10–20 ml aliquots and stop for cough, wet voice or fatigue; if the swallow is unsafe, use tube flushes or a lower density. These are the numbers that make “more calories” and “enough water” compatible.
  4. Add one between-meal supplement only after the two-week trial has ended and total intake is still below 110 kcal/kg. Start at 5–10 ml/kg (maximum 100 ml) of 1.0–1.5 kcal/ml ONS, 30–60 minutes after a meal or before bed. Do not stack ONS on top of the density trial: the ceiling is 840 kcal/day (140 kcal/kg). Track total intake, not supplement volume. If the next meal falls by more than the supplement, the timing or volume is wrong.
  5. Treat the limiters. Reflux, constipation, dental pain, oral motor fatigue and positioning. A speech-language pathologist or occupational therapist should see the child before the next density increase if there is any cough, wet voice or feeding fatigue.
  6. Escalate on a defined trigger. If weight is falling across two consecutive WHS-chart measurements, if intake is below 80% of estimated need for 2–4 weeks despite the above, or if there is no catch-up gain over 2–4 weeks despite documented intake, discuss NG feeding. Define failure numerically: weight gain <5 g/kg/day averaged over 14 days (<30 g/day at 6 kg; poor on WHO rehabilitation categories, 5–10 g/kg/day moderate), weight velocity <−2 z-score or a decline of ≥1 z-score on the WHS chart, a fall of more than 5% in a week, or a downward crossing of two major WHS centile lines [1][2]. Stable weight with poor linear growth is a failure of the caloric hypothesis, not a success. After the NICE NG75 gate (specialist multidisciplinary assessment and other interventions tried), if enteral nutrition is expected to be needed for more than 4–6 weeks, a gastrostomy is generally preferred to a prolonged nasogastric tube; discuss the timing with the team [104]. The tube guarantees delivered energy, not total energy: oral intake may fall, so measure tube plus oral intake after any change.
  7. Refeed safely. Baseline and 24–72 hour phosphate, potassium and magnesium; thiamine if high risk; advance by plan.

What is extrapolated and what is confirmed. Confirmed in WHS: prenatal growth deficiency, hypotonia, feeding difficulty, dysphagia in case series, and the use of gastrostomy in severe cases. Extrapolated from other populations: the 48.7% COMPX, the 5.4% per 10% density slope, the ONS weight effect, and the tube-weaning rates. There is no WHS-specific trial of energy density or compensation. The plan above is a reasoned application of general evidence to a specific child; it should be treated as a hypothesis with measurement, not as a syndrome-specific protocol.

In plain words. In WHS, growth is partly programmed before birth. More calories may not mean more height. The plan is a test: measure intake and growth, and look for absorption or hormone problems if weight does not respond.

8. One-page care-team protocol

Step 1 — Define the limiter. Record weight-for-length/BMI-for-age, MUAC, oedema and the WHS-chart centile, and state explicitly whether WHO severe acute malnutrition criteria are met; if they are, the WHO SAM pathway applies alongside the ASPEN refeeding bundle. Before any change, confirm renal ultrasound and function, urinalysis, electrolytes including sodium and bicarbonate, and cardiac status with any fluid restriction. Then identify the limiter: safety (aspiration), motility (vomiting, early fullness), sensory (repertoire), energy need (CHD, CF, SBS), inflammation/obstruction (EoE, stricture), or psychosocial. The limiter decides the first move.

Step 2 — Measure. 3-day weighed intake; weight, length, head circumference on the condition-specific chart; urine output; sodium, phosphate, potassium, magnesium; stool pattern; feeding observation by SLP/OT if any cough or fatigue.

Step 3 — Optimise oral density. Increase kcal/ml, not ml/feed. Fat/MCT first where tolerated; small frequent feeds; treat reflux/constipation/pain. Keep a fixed volume target for two weeks so the compensation response is visible; the density ceiling is 1.22 kcal/ml at 500 ml; 840 kcal/day is the full-solids total at that density, not a separate limit. Before any oral free water, obtain an SLP/OT assessment and a documented consistency plan; give water in 10–20 ml aliquots and stop for cough, wet voice or fatigue. If the swallow is unsafe, use tube flushes or a lower density rather than thin oral water.

Step 4 — Add between-meal calories. Start at 5–10 ml/kg (maximum 100 ml) of 1.0–1.5 kcal/ml ONS only after the two-week density trial and only if total intake remains below 110 kcal/kg. Give it 30–60 min after a meal; avoid the before-bed dose if reflux or aspiration is active, and step up only if the next meal is preserved. The +0.4 kg meta-analytic effect comes from about 400 ml/day in 5-year-olds and is industry-funded; it is not a dose-response for 100 ml in an 18-month-old. Recheck the next meal; if it falls by more than the supplement, change timing or reduce volume.

Step 5 — Protect water. Holliday–Segar maintenance (100/50/20 ml/kg/day) plus losses. At 6 kg: 600 ml/day of total water from all sources, not 600 ml of free water. At the prescribed 500 ml: 1.0 kcal/ml gives about 425 ml water from milk, so the maximum supplemental free water is about 175 ml; at 1.22 kcal/ml it is about 390 ml from milk and about 210 ml free water. If energy is held and the volume falls to 361 ml at 1.0 kcal/ml, the maximum is about 293 ml (420 ml at 1.5 kcal/ml) — these are full-compensation upper bounds, not the prescribed-volume plan. Give water between feeds; monitor urine output, specific gravity and sodium. These are maximum supplemental volumes assuming no water from solids; start at 50–100 ml/day and titrate to urine output, specific gravity and sodium. Do not use 1.5 kcal/ml in this protocol; the ceiling is 1.22 kcal/ml. In dysphagia, respect consistency and aspiration risk: SLP/OT assessment, thickened or small-aliquot oral water, or tube flushes if oral water is unsafe.

Step 6 — Escalate on a trigger. Weight falling on two consecutive chart measurements, intake <80% of need for 2–4 weeks, or no catch-up gain over 2–4 weeks despite documented intake → NG trial. Define failure numerically: weight gain <5 g/kg/day averaged over 14 days (<30 g/day at 6 kg; poor on WHO rehabilitation categories, 5–10 g/kg/day moderate), weight velocity <−2 z-score or a decline of ≥1 z-score on the WHS chart, a fall of more than 5% in a week, or a downward crossing of two major WHS centile lines [1][2]. Stable weight with poor linear growth is a failure of the caloric hypothesis, not a success. After the NICE NG75 gate (specialist multidisciplinary assessment and other interventions tried), if enteral nutrition is expected to be needed for more than 4–6 weeks, a gastrostomy is generally preferred to a prolonged nasogastric tube [104]. Tube feeding is a caloric route, not a failure.

Step 7 — Refeed and review. Baseline and 24–72 h phosphate/K/Mg; give thiamine 2 mg/kg (max 100–200 mg) if high risk. For high risk start at the current tolerated intake if the child is already eating; otherwise start at 40–50% of the goal (ASPEN) and use 50–60% only with a documented reason. For moderate risk start at 75–80% of the goal. In the oral density trial use the 3–5 day ramp; the 10–20 kcal/kg/day refeeding schedule applies only to the malnourished/high-risk refeeding pathway. ASPEN grades a 10–20% fall in phosphate/potassium/magnesium as mild, 20–30% as moderate and >30% as severe. Week 1: days 1–3 keep the current volume and density while baseline intake, weight, urine and sodium are recorded; days 4–7 ramp from 0.72 to 1.0 to 1.22 kcal/ml if tolerated. Review weight, intake, water, urine specific gravity and tolerance daily in week 1, then weekly; check sodium at baseline, 24–72 h after a density change, then weekly. De-escalate the tube only within a structured weaning programme.

Red flags. Coughing or choking with feeds, wet voice, recurrent pneumonia, desaturation, feeding time >30 minutes with fatigue, blood in stool, bilious vomiting, severe abdominal pain, weight loss >5% in a week, sodium <135 mmol/L (dilutional) or >145 mmol/L, or new oedema. A fall in phosphate, potassium or magnesium of 10–20% or more within 5 days of starting or increasing nutrition is an ASPEN red flag [131]. Interpret any value against the age-specific lower limit; paediatric reference intervals are age-partitioned [140][141]. These are reasons to stop escalating orally and involve the medical team the same day.

In plain words. Find the blocker, measure, make every mouthful count, add calories between meals, protect water, and move to a tube if weight keeps falling.

9. Methods, reproducibility and limitations

9.1 Search and screening

PubMed was searched on 19 November 2026 with 43 core queries and 15 supplemental queries (58 total), covering feeding-disorder taxonomy and populations, caloric/energy compensation, energy density, oral nutritional supplements and appetite stimulants, water and renal solute load, refeeding, and WHS-specific terms. The 58 queries returned 29,525 hits; 2,144 were retrieved in the original core harvest. Round 6 then re-ran the 15 clinical queries (1,290/1,290), the 10 queries feeding the pooled COMPX/energy-density estimates (2,925/2,925) and all 12 recency queries (1,467/1,467) without a retmax cap; 3,571 + 1,150 new unique records remained after de-duplication. Five additional records came from targeted WHS and paediatric reference-interval lookups, giving 6,600 unique records in data/records.json. For the 48 narrative/map queries the original relevance cap was retained: 1,816 of 26,600 hits were screened (top 40 per core query, top 60 per supplemental query). The original 1,340-record set was triaged with a local classifier and checked manually; the remaining records were screened through the keyword prefilter and checked manually. Inclusion required an experimental or controlled human study with an objective intake outcome and extractable variance; animal, mechanistic and non-quantitative records were excluded. The exhaustive Round-6 screen added one COMPX study (Hetherington 2000, PMID 27414130) and two energy-density studies (Sanchez-Griñan 1992, PMID 1559524; Islam 2006, PMID 16600938); no further eligible COMPX or energy-density trial was found.

9.2 Extraction and one-effect-per-study rule

COMPX and energy-density data were extracted into data/extraction_compx.csv and data/extraction_ed.csv. The primary analysis uses one effect per study. The rule was fixed before analysis: for a study that measured the same children at two ages, use the later age (Brugaillères 2019, 15 months); for a study with two preload tests in the same children, use Test A, the iso-caloric beverage with the larger sample (Carnell 2017); for a study with separate strata (Cecil 2005 age groups; Kasese-Hara 2002 cases/controls), combine strata by fixed-effect inverse variance; the Kral 2012 sibling strata are combined approximately because siblings are correlated, and the row carries little weight. The split rows are reported as a sensitivity analysis. For the five-day energy-density study, the primary analysis uses the higher-density arm, the direction that answers the clinical question; the lower-density arm is in the split sensitivity.

9.3 Statistics

Random-effects meta-analysis used the DerSimonian–Laird estimator of τ², with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman confidence intervals as the primary interval and DL intervals reported for convention. Heterogeneity is reported as I² and τ²; prediction intervals are reported for both pools. Subgroups were tested with a mixed-effects Q-between centred on the inverse-variance weighted mean of subgroup means. Leave-one-out analyses are in analysis/results_compx.json and analysis/results_ed.json. Egger’s test was pre-specified only for k≥10 and was not run for either pool. A weighted meta-regression of COMPX on mean age was run as an exploratory analysis, with τ² held at the intercept-only DL estimate. The code is analysis/meta.py; sensitivity analyses are analysis/sensitivity.py.

9.4 Evidence grades used in this report

A systematic review/meta-analysis or consistent RCTs; B controlled trials with limitations, cohort studies, or consistent physiology plus observational data; C case series, expert consensus, or indirect evidence; D physiological reasoning without direct human data. The grade reflects the strength of the evidence for the specific claim, not the prestige of the source.

9.5 Limitations

  • The COMPX pool is small (14 studies), heterogeneous (I² 72.4%), and almost entirely composed of healthy children in laboratory settings. It does not include children with WHS, and only one small cohort is undernourished.
  • COMPX is an acute single-meal preload measure. “Net kcal kept” is an extrapolation to daily intake, not a measured daily effect; the energy-density trials provide a convergent but not identical estimate.
  • The energy-density pool is seven studies, mostly single meals in preschool children; the five-day trial is the strongest design but still small (n=49).
  • Publication bias could not be assessed reliably; negative or null studies may be underrepresented.
  • No individual participant data; no formal risk-of-bias tool was applied to each study; the extraction was single-reviewer with a reproducibility file, not dual independent extraction.
  • The WHS feeding literature is thin: feeding difficulty is described, but syndrome-specific prevalence, compensation and tube-weaning data are lacking. The WHS section is therefore an extrapolation, explicitly labelled.
  • The blinded second-screen sample found 31/32 included studies and 17 disagreements; after adjudication, three eligible clinical trials were added, but the observed missed rates imply about 25 further eligible reports among the 3,376 prefiltered exclusions (rule-of-three ceiling 25) and about 16 among the 3,192 prefilter failures (ceiling 48). The clinical table is therefore not exhaustive, and the pooled COMPX/ED estimates rest on the nine/seven studies with extractable data, not on a claim that no other study exists.
  • The report is a research synthesis, not medical advice. Doses, feeding plans and tube decisions must be individualised by the treating team.
