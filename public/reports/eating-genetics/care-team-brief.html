What the evidence says — a short brief for the care team

v0.1.1 — interim living review, evidence date 2026-11-21

Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome, 18 months · a sourced brief · not advice and not a plan

Who and what

A boy of 18 months with Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome (4p16.3 deletion), 6.0 kg, taking about 590 kcal/day (98.3 kcal/kg/day). Evidence date: 2026-11-21. This document says what the evidence says — it is not advice and not a plan; decisions belong to the care team.

Growth on WHS charts

On the WHS charts (Antonius 2008, n = 101; Calhoun 2025) he sits at about the 22nd–34th centile. WHO charts give lower centiles and are not the right reference in WHS. Length is missing: without it neither weight-for-length nor a length-based catch-up target can be calculated.

Two intake framings

His current intake is 98.3 kcal/kg/day; typical maintenance for age is 82.4 kcal/kg, so he is 19% above maintenance. In the frozen report 890 kcal/day is an age-based illustration that exceeds its own 840 kcal/day ceiling and is labelled an illustration, not a target. A length-based target cannot be calculated without his length — so both framings depend on that measurement.

What the evidence would predict

The numbers are arithmetic on published effects under the stated assumptions, not a plan. Fixed-volume density comes first because his pattern is held kcal with falling volume; at free volume the expected gain for him is close to zero. Weight-only levers carry no kcal row because the trials do not report baseline weight.

First-line options

Feeding programmes: 71% of children were weaned off the tube (CI 54–83). Meal frequency: four meals rather than three gave +16% of total daily intake in one trial without a CI. Oral supplements: 450 kcal/day delivered, not net; no comparator and no baseline. Tube feeding: in WHS it is about one in four children by age 5; in the largest paediatric study oral intake did not fall on average after enteral feeding began (Zambelli 2024), but that study was retrospective and not WHS, so oral intake needs measuring if it is started.

Medicines

All are off-label at 18 months — in Poland and in the US. Cyproheptadine: small weight gain in small trials (0.69 kg, CI 0.12–1.25), but sedation and anticholinergic effects matter in WHS with epilepsy. Megestrol: adrenal suppression in 10 of 11 children tested (PMID 12872362), thromboembolism in the label, no paediatric authorisation (in PL the dose is not established below 12 years). Mirtazapine: sedation, adult data only. Cannabinoids: cannabidiol (Epidyolex) is authorised in the EU as adjunctive therapy for Dravet and Lennox–Gastaut syndromes and tuberous sclerosis from age 2 years, and interacts with clobazam and valproate; THC/dronabinol is a separate matter with no paediatric authorisation.

What would change the answers

Four measurements would change the conclusions most: length on a WHS chart (unlocks weight-for-length and the catch-up target); intake measured with volume held (separates compensation from poor appetite); a growth target based on WHS data, not WHO; and exclusion of imitators — IDUA/MPS I only if features are not explained by WHS.

Questions for the care team

  1. What is his length on the WHS chart, and what catch-up target follows from it?
  2. Is 98.3 kcal/kg/day too low or adequate given his length?
  3. If feeds are densified, what is the plan for water intake and osmolality checks?
  4. Would measuring intake at a fixed volume separate compensation from poor appetite?
  5. Which first-line options are realistic here, and what will we measure to judge the effect?
  6. If a medicine is considered, what are the specific epilepsy concerns and monitoring tests?
  7. Which features would justify testing for IDUA/MPS I despite the WHS diagnosis?
  8. What would have to change for the current feeding approach to count as insufficient?
