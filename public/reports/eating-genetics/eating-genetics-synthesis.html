1. Executive summary

Evidence date: 2026-11-21

This living review asks which genes change eating behaviour, how those genes have been turned into methods that raise or lower caloric intake, and what the resulting evidence means for one child: a boy with a Wolf–Hirschhorn (4p16.3) deletion, 18 months old and 6.0 kg, taking about 590 kcal/day, with 41 levers in the table.

Three findings carry the review. First, the lowering side is far better evidenced than the raising side: GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce ad libitum test-meal intake by −29.8% (95% CI −39.9 to −19.7, k = 4, I² = 0%), and this is the only lowering class with a precise pool. Second, the largest raising-side pool is small and exogenous: intravenous ghrelin raises intake by 30.8% per test meal (14.3 to 47.3, k = 3), and no oral, paediatric ghrelin agonist has a variance we can pool. Third, in children, cyproheptadine buys about 0.69 kg (0.12 to 1.25 at a pre-post correlation of 0.9, k = 3), an estimate whose significance depends on an assumption no trial reports.

The feeding-disorders cross-check is a separate result: the frozen project's pooled estimates stand, and uncited paediatric appetite-stimulant and weaning evidence is listed in UPDATE-CANDIDATES.md. For the index child the report is deliberately not a plan. It states what is known, what is not, and which measurement would settle the open question — his length on a Wolf–Hirschhorn chart — and leaves the decision to the care team.

The raising side has one reporting pool and several plausible single studies. Intravenous ghrelin raises test-meal intake by 30.8% (14.3 to 47.3, k = 3, I² = 14%), from three adult crossover studies; the 2016 review's SMD of 2.67 was not used because it is implausible (k = 8, I² near 90%, Egger P = 0.037).

In children, cyproheptadine is the only raising drug with three trials and derivable variances: 0.69 kg (0.12 to 1.25) at r = 0.9, and the sensitivity table in the CSV shows the interval crossing zero at r = 0.7 and below. Behavioural and dietary levers are first-line at this age and carry no licence: intensive feeding programmes (one RCT with d = 1.03–2.11; a meta-analysis of 11 programmes giving 71% weaned, 95% CI 54–83%) [15] [16], meal frequency (+16% on four versus three meals in 6–18-month children) [17], oral supplements (450 kcal/day delivered in a single-arm trial) [18], and responsive feeding, whose only trial changed self-feeding behaviour without increasing weight gain [19].

For the index child none of these is "applies": the curated states are "plausible", and the reasons name the age, the population and the label jurisdiction.

The lowering side is the better-evidenced half, which is the opposite of what this child needs. GLP-1 receptor agonists lower ad libitum test-meal intake by −29.8% (−39.9 to −19.7, k = 4) — −279.6 kcal per meal on the kcal scale (−378.9 to −180.3) — mostly in adults, with one kcal-pool trial in hypothalamic obesity from age 10–26 and semaglutide supplying three of the four percent-pool trials. Whole-day intake is heterogeneous: pooled −635.3 kcal/day (−1401.3 to 130.8, k = 4, I² = 91%), an interval that crosses zero, so it is reported but not used for decisions.

In clinical hyperphagia the class effect is small and imprecise. Oxytocin-receptor agonists give a standardised mean difference of −0.21 (−0.59 to 0.18, k = 3), and the k = 4 sensitivity including the one oxytocin trial with an imputed SD is −0.14 (−0.47 to 0.19); one molecule points the wrong way. Diazoxide's parallel and withdrawal trials are reported separately because they answer different questions. In binge-eating disorder the outcomes are binge frequency and abstinence, not daily intake: CBT against waitlist gives a remission risk ratio of 3.21 and a binge-frequency SMD of −0.88 [20], while CBT for weight loss in obesity gives -1.7 kg [21]. Nothing here is framed as a way to restrict food.

6. The index case: Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome at 18 months

Evidence date: 2026-11-21

Care-team brief. The same material as a two-page brief for the people around the child: care-team-brief.html (EN) and care-team-brief-PL.html (PL).

Growth. Standard charts are inapplicable. On the digitised Antonius 2008 boys' lines (n = 101) his 6.0 kg at 18 months is z = −0.77, about the 22nd centile; on the combined-sex Calhoun 2025 curves it is about the 34th centile. On WHO charts the same weight is far below normal. Length is unknown, so weight-for-length cannot be computed.

Intake, two framings. His 98.3 kcal/kg/day is about 19% above the FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 maintenance figure of 82.4 kcal/kg/day for boys aged 1–2 years. Against that, the frozen feeding-disorders report's length-based catch-up formula cannot be computed without his length; its age-based illustration reaches about 890 kcal/day, above its own 840 kcal/day ceiling, and is labelled an illustration rather than a target. The two framings sit side by side because they point in different directions, and the measurement that settles which applies is his length on a WHS chart.

Dysphagia, by analogy. No WHS series quantifies it, so the nearest evidence comes from syndromes with the same early feeding phenotype: in 983 females with Rett syndrome, chewing and swallowing difficulty and gastrostomy varied by MECP2 status [22], and in 15 CHD7-positive CHARGE patients 12 had swallowing problems [23]. These describe other conditions and cannot be transferred to this child; they mark dysphagia as a syndrome-level risk that a feeding assessment should look for rather than a WHS-specific rate.

Medication. Valproate raised weight by 0.49 kg in three weeks in 52 healthy adults without changing measured intake, so weight change on it is not an intake signal [24]; seizures and sedation matter more than appetite when a drug like cyproheptadine is weighed. Growth hormone is reported only in single cases, the longest being 11 years with height from about −4.2 to −1.3 SDS [25].

7. What the levers would mean for him, if the evidence transferred

Evidence date: 2026-11-21

This section turns the raising levers into his numbers. It is arithmetic on published effects under five stated assumptions (project data files), not a plan: the care team decides. Two scenarios matter for energy density. At fixed volume — the pattern he already shows, holding kcal while dropping volume — the frozen paediatric studies predict 93.5 kcal/day, with a conservative floor of 20 and an upper end of 105.0. At free volume the typical-child elasticity would give 31.9 kcal/day at +10% density (20.4 to 43.5), but for him that route is expected to be about zero because the volume is what he drops. The ghrelin pool is a single-meal measure, so it goes through the frozen whole-day retention and becomes 59.9 kcal/day (11.8 to 145.0) — and that assumes every meal is boosted, which a single infusion does not establish. The supplement trial delivered 450 kcal/day without a comparator or baseline, so no net figure is given, and the adult drug trials report kcal without their own baseline intake, so they are not scaled to him either. Weight-only levers carry no kcal row at all: without the trials' mean baseline weight, a percentage of his body weight would overstate what those gains mean.

Caveat. The largest predicted numbers come from the weakest evidence, and several rows carry no kcal figure on purpose. Nothing here says a lever will work in him: the syndrome modifies absorption, growth and feeding skill, and no source study measured any of that. The table exists so the care team can weigh the options with their sizes attached, not so that any one of them is chosen.

For an 18-month-old these are the first-line options: they need no licence, no off-label decision and no blood test, and the only one with a label-grade constraint is energy density, which works only if the prescribed volume is held. The table gives each lever's effect as the extracted rows report it, the population and age those rows cover, the GRADE rating for intake and the curated index-case line.

These levers do not act on a gene or a receptor but are the ones a family can use first. Energy density raises intake only if the prescribed volume is held: pooled preload experiments give +5.41% intake per +10% density (k = 7), and whole-day retention is 33–42%, so about half to two-thirds of added energy is compensated at later meals. Meal frequency adds 16% on four versus three meals [17]. Intensive feeding programmes are the only intervention aimed at food refusal itself [15] [16], oral supplements deliver a fixed 450 kcal/day without a demonstrated net gain [18], and responsive feeding changed behaviour without changing weight [19]. Tube feeding is a route, not a retention strategy; weaning programmes pooled across 11 studies report 71% success (95% CI 54–83) [16], while single programmes report 72.5% (37/51) [26] and 33.3% at discharge rising to 78% at follow-up [27].

10. Methods

Evidence date: 2026-11-21

Search. 63,179 unique records were harvested from PubMed and Europe PMC across the project's query set, plus the recency sweep. Prefilter assigns priority 12,238, 27,020 and 23,836 by title and abstract pattern; screening then reads the abstract in waves of four and records include, exclude or borderline with a reason code.

Screening status. 7,600 records have a decision (12.0% of the corpus): 1,064 includes, 6,287 excludes, 249 borderline, across 78 batch files. The PRISMA flow is therefore interim, not final.

Extraction and checks. Extracted rows live in project data files; each carries the verbatim quote, unit, time base, estimator and variance route. check_levers.py enforces 14 rules, including value-in-quote, derived-value reconstruction, unit-versus-quote, time base on every numeric row, design claims visible in the quote, p-value back-calculation, CI bracketing, percentage arithmetic, pool class purity and the curated index-case state. Pools use DerSimonian–Laird with the modified Knapp–Hartung interval as primary when k is below five, and are reported only at k ≥ 3; k = 2 is descriptive.

Full-text step. Of the 83 index-case queue records, 19 yielded a full text and 64 did not. Of the 83 records, 27 carry a PMCID but only those in the open-access subset can be fetched: the 8 that failed — [28] [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] [35] — are in PMC with isOpenAccess = N, so they are marked no open full text, not no data.

Rules the checker enforces. 5a value present for every numeric row; 5b a CI brackets its own estimate and names an estimator; 5b1 time base on every numeric intake or weight row; 5b2 p-value back-calculation from t = |y|/se; 5c no blank grade cells; 5d a randomisation claim visible in the row's own quote; 5e applies reserved for direct index-population evidence with no label barrier; 5f quote verbatim from the abstract; 5g every PMID exists in the harvested corpus; 5h no meta-analysis inside a primary pool; 5i one class per pool unless flagged as an illustration; 5j k/n percentage arithmetic; 5k curated index-case state identical across lever-meta, caloric-levers and INDEX-CASE; 5l any claim that a gene lies in the 4p16 deletion, or that a pathway is intact or absent because of it, must name a gene in project data files or state that the locus lies outside it.

Meta-analysis. DerSimonian–Laird with the modified Knapp–Hartung interval as primary below five studies; standard errors from the CI, the exact p or the arm SD/SEM by the Cochrane routes, with a crossover correlation of 0.5 and 0.3/0.7 sensitivity; one effect per study under the protocol arm rule (highest dose, arms combined by Cochrane 6.5.2.10 where populations differ).

Blinded second screen. A fresh screener re-screened all includes plus a stratified random sample of excludes from title and abstract only, without access to the first decisions: 1,307 records so far. Agreement with the first screen is reported raw and after one documented mapping (`EX_* → exclude`, `IN_* → include`, `BL_* → borderline`): raw kappa 0.57, mapped 0.53, and restricted to records outside the codes tightened mid-run 0.75 (n = 855). The raw-to-mapped gap is the narrative-review convention — `IN_DRUG`/`IN_MECH` against `EX_NARRATIVE_REVIEW` — not general disagreement; the full disagreement table and adjudication are in project data files

Screen sensitivity. The blinded second screen gives an estimable false-exclusion rate. Of 400 first-screen excludes in its sample, 12 were confirmed includes after a full-text re-read — 3.00% (Wilson 95% CI 1.72–5.17); by tier, 1 of 58 in priority 1 (1.72%) and 11 of 342 in priority 2 (3.22%). Applied to the 5,010 first-screen excludes, that implies about 150 missed includes (range 86–259). The remaining priority-1 screening uses the same prompt and the same screener pool, so the same rate is assumed there.

Citations and parity. Every PMID in this report is resolved through project data files, and the EN and PL reports are generated from one source with a parity check on section ids, citation order and numeric tokens.

11. Limitations

Evidence date: 2026-11-21

A queue bug, fixed 2026-11-21. Until 2026-11-21 the batch builder sorted with a descending priority key, so priority 2 preceded priority 1: every main batch was drawn from priority 2 and the 12,238 priority-1 records were never queued. Only 216 of them had been screened through the intake-priority batches; the p1 queue has since screened 2,400 of them, yielding 542 includes. The sort is fixed and pinned by a test, and 12,238 priority-1 records are now queued as p1-0000 onward; the priority-2 decisions already made remain valid.

Screening depth. Only 12.0% of the 63,179-record corpus has a screening decision. The lever table is therefore built on a documented but incomplete screen, and the PRISMA flow is interim.

Second screen, completed in part. A blinded second screen re-read 1,307 records (all includes plus a stratified sample of excludes), in 14 batches: raw kappa 0.57, mapped 0.53, restricted 0.75. Of 301 include/exclude disagreements, 194 are narrative-review calls that the protocol resolves as exclusions, 17 are records the first pass excluded and the blinded pass would include (re-read at full text before any extraction), and the rest are other exclusions or borderline calls. The 0.698 quoted earlier was truncated-versus-full-abstract agreement, not inter-rater reliability. All 14 batches are in, so the kappa is final for this screen.

Full-text access. Of the 83 index-case queue records, 19 yielded an open full text and 64 are closed access; those were marked "no open full text", not "no data".

Evidence gaps. No WHS energy-requirement or intake study exists in 383 WHS records (0 mention intake). The raising side has one reporting intake pool (IV ghrelin, adults); the paediatric cyproheptadine pool is weight, and it depends on an assumption. Several key dispersions are unpublished, which is why two k = 2 pools are descriptive. The index child's length is unknown, which blocks weight-for-length and the length-based catch-up target.

Incomplete detection. The rate above implies roughly one in 3333 excluded records would survive a full read — about 150 records (range 86–259) across the screened corpus, a number that grows with the corpus. None of the 12 confirmed misses changes a pooled estimate: the GLP-1 network meta-analysis and the semaglutide review report weight, not intake, so the GLP-1 receptor agonist intake pool is unchanged, and the mechanism records add lever context without an intake effect.

What this document is not. It is not medical advice and not a feeding plan. It is a sourced map for the care team to decide from.
